# Dogfooding `vas offers --watch`, 2026-10-18 — the market, watched

The morning after `--watch` was written, Éric ran both modes on the live market, with the askesis filters:
`vas offers --watch 30` (the whole table, marked) and `vas offers --watch 30 --changes` (only what moves).

## What it showed

- **It explains why renting is a nightmare.** Boxes go away and new ones arrive by the second: the list a
  person decides from is already out of date when the decision is made. Éric: "so useful".
- Both ways were worth having: the marked table to decide from, `--changes` to see the churn itself.

## An idea for later: a TUI for the watch

The line-by-line output is the honest minimum; the watch wants a window of its own: text-based, redrawn in
place, with colour. Not for now (the text modes stay: they are what scripts, logs and `ssh` sessions
without a terminal need). Sketched so the idea is kept, not designed:

- **Redrawn in place**, on the alternate screen, instead of a new table printed under the old one.
- **Colour that means something:** `+` new in green, a price down in green and up in red, `(was …)` dimmed;
  `NO_COLOR` respected; the same text when the output is not a terminal.
- **Rows that linger.** An offer that just vanished stays a few looks, struck through or greyed, where it
  was: the point of watching is to see what you just missed, and the text modes can only list it below.
- **Keys:** quit, pause (freeze the list to read it), sort, `+`/`-` to change the interval, mark an offer to
  follow (its price history, and when it disappears).
- **A trend.** The cheapest price over the last looks as a sparkline; how many offers entered and left per
  look: the churn as a number.
- **Shape of the work:** behind a cargo feature (`tui`: a terminal-UI crate such as ratatui, with a backend
  such as crossterm), so the default install keeps its few dependencies; Windows Terminal, macOS and Linux
  to be checked in CI; the lookout (`vas --watch`) could share it, with the GPU trend drawn the same way.

Open questions:

- **The refresh rate.** 30 s is a floor chosen not to hammer a public service, and Éric's "by the second"
  suggests it is coarse for this market. A TUI could poll faster, but that is a decision about being a good
  citizen of vast.ai's search, not about drawing; to settle before any faster default (the answer may
  be a floor of 10 to 15 s, said in the footer).
- **Whether a TUI belongs in `vas` at all** or in a sibling binary, given the read-only, small-dependency
  character of the crate.

## The power cap (2026-10-18, evening)

The two 5090s rented that day are capped by their host (reported for both; read directly over `ssh` on one): `nvidia-smi` read a power limit of 400 W
against the card's default of 575 W (`power.default_limit`), a draw of 399.86 W, a SM clock of 1695-1702 MHz
against a maximum of 3090 MHz, and `clocks_throttle_reasons.active` = `0x4`, the software power cap, with the
GPU at 100 % utilisation. Nothing in the dashboard, in `vas`, or in the offer listing says so: a rented
"RTX 5090" may run at a fraction of what the model can do, and a 100 % reading hides it. `vas --ssh` now
reads it (a `POWER` column, `power_cap` in the JSON); whether the listing's price should be weighed by it is
for whoever rents. Not measured: how much slower the training runs for it.

## Someone else on the GPU (2026-10-18, evening)

One of the two boxes ran slower. `nvidia-smi` read 24,809 MiB in use on the card, while the only process it
listed (our training) held 8,720 MiB, `hmn ps` agreed, and `lsof`/`fuser` on `/dev/nvidia*` and the processes
that map `libcuda` showed nothing else in the container: about 16 GiB belonged to something outside it.
`nvidia-smi pmon` gave our process 24-82 % of the SM (about 50 % on average) in ten one-second samples while the
card read 100 %, and the SM clock dipped from 1,695 to about 1,480 MHz. The other box (another machine, same
workload) added up (8,772 of 8,782 MiB), held 98-99 % of the SM and a steady clock. Its driver was newer
(595.71.05 against 580.159.03); whether that matters is not known. The cause is not known either: another tenant
of the same GPU, or the host's own work. It was reported to vast.ai support. `vas --ssh` now shows the gap
(`OTHER`) and a watch says when it appears. Not included: the process's share of the SM against the card's
utilisation, which takes a few seconds of sampling.

## The rate limit, found by a latency script (2026-10-18, evening)

A script that ran `vas` a dozen times in a minute, to time the account request overlapping the `ssh`
probes, was answered `429` on its second call: `vast.ai returned status 429 for /api/v1/instances/
(rate-limited: wait before the next call)`. `vas` did what it was built to do (it said so, with the hint),
but it knew nothing of the budget.

vast.ai publishes it in the answers' headers, per endpoint:

- the public offer search (`/api/v0/bundles/`, no key): `X-RateLimit-Limit: 30.0`, `Remaining: 29`, a reset
  about 2 s after the first call of a quiet period;
- the account's instances list (`/api/v1/instances/`, with the key): `X-RateLimit-Limit: 1`, `Remaining: 0`
  right after Éric's single call, with a `Reset` time.

One call per window on the endpoint a lookout calls every look. The window's length was not yet known (a
loop printing the headers every 8 s was asked for); the `--watch` floor of 30 s was a guess made before any of
this, and Me36's `--watch 120` was safe.

What `vas` does about it now, and why in the binary: it reads the headers, waits out a refusal of 15 s or
less once and says so, names a longer one, and paces a watch by the budget's reset. The library only reports.
The floor itself is left alone until the window is measured.

## Correction: the offer list is a flickering sample, not a market (2026-10-18, afternoon)

The morning's reaction to `vas offers --watch` ("boxes are going away and new ones are coming by the second")
was read too literally, and so was my reading of it. Run for three minutes (`--watch 30 --changes`, the
askesis filters), it reported about 17 new and 17 gone out of 33 to 35 offers on EVERY look: half the list
turning over each 30 s, with pairs such as the Taiwan 0.766 offer alternating between ids `34376033` and
`34376038`, and a Hungary machine cycling through eight ids.

Measured against the public search afterwards (read-only, nothing of the account):

- Fifteen identical searches 2.5 s apart (RTX 5090, 1 GPU, 64 rows each): 129 distinct machines in all, only
  30 present in all fifteen; the others spread evenly from 1 to 14 looks. The cheapest row never changed
  (0.414); the 64th ranged 0.88 to 0.93, so a machine at 0.70 is absent from some answers although it is far
  under the cut.
- Not the cap: with stricter filters the answers had 35 rows (under 64) and still flipped by about 40 %.
- Not the GET form: the official CLI's POST form, which also answers without a key, was as unstable (31 % of
  the machines in every look, against 33 % for GET).
- Offer ids are not an identity: 11 of 75 machines appeared under two or three ids within five looks.

A hypothesis, NOT proven: a machine is listed only while its host's report is fresh, so slow reporters
flicker in and out, and the always-present 30 are the dependable hosts.

So: one search is a sample; two searches differ by about a third; and a watch that compares offer ids reports
the sampling, not the market. The real churn (a machine rented, a price changed) is in there somewhere, but
cannot be read off that output. The watch is to be redone: identity by machine, a `SEEN` count per machine,
marks debounced over several looks, and every `vas offers` footer saying that answers vary.
